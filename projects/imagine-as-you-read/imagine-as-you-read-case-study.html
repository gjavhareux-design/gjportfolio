Chapter one · Introduction

Meet Imagine As You Read

Why reading needed a visual companion.

An MSc major project. Imagine As You Read was my major project (CI7801) for the MSc in User Experience Design at Kingston University London. It went from research to a tested high-fidelity Figma prototype.

The challenge

Text-only reading isn't reaching readers.

The problem, and the idea that answered it

Reading is declining. Stories don't matter less; text-only formats just don't meet readers where they are, cognitively or visually.

The core problem

  • Digitised, not transformed: Digital tools put text on screens without changing the experience of reading it.
  • No help to imagine: Readers who struggle to picture scenes have nothing beyond the same wall of text.
  • Fragile focus: Long-form reading competes with constant digital distraction.
Problem statement

Readers who struggle to visualise, stay focused or connect emotionally with a story get no meaningful support from today's reading tools.

The design solution

  • Visuals on demand: Generative AI turns a selected passage into a contextual visual scene.
  • User-triggered: Nothing appears unless the reader asks for it, and it can be switched off at any time.
  • Imagination, not replacement: Visuals support the act of reading; they never replace it.
Design principle

The reader decides when, and how much, AI appears.

Approach

Think, make, check.

A Lean UX loop from research to tested prototype

Each loop answered one question before the next one started.

Think · Research

Literature review, 8 user interviews and a survey with 66 responses.

Make · Design

Card sorting and information architecture, a lo-fi paper prototype and a hi-fi Figma prototype.

Check · Test

Usability testing, hypothesis validation and weighted scoring.

The most important design decision

"The most important design decision was the one I didn't make: choosing not to make visual generation automatic, resisting the temptation to showcase AI capability. Research consistently said: give users control."

Information architecture

Reading first, visuals on request.

Progressive disclosure, so readers choose how deep to go

Half of survey respondents wanted "text with optional visuals". Hiding visual features inside the reading view would have broken that expectation, so the experience is split into clear modes, and every step into AI is the reader's choice.

Core reading flow

Reader in control at every step
1Read in text-first mode
▶
2Select a passage
▶
3Generate a visual in the side panel
▶
4Adjust intensity, regenerate or dismiss
▶
Back to reading

Usability testing

Three passes, and one useful failure.

Core tasks, scored on time, errors and assistance

Testing ran across every fidelity. The one failed task pointed straight at the biggest design flaw.

ParticipantTaskTimeErrorsAssistanceResult
P1Enable visual mode32 s00✓ Pass

P4Use immersive mode55 s21✗ Fail
Relief

Participants said, without being asked, that they were relieved to hear visual generation was optional.

Control

Every participant used the "dismiss visual" control, and used it with enthusiasm. People needed the override.

Design iterations

Every change traces to a friction point.

Two revisions driven by what we observed

1

Immersive mode: hard to discover

Before

Immersive mode sat in the mode switcher with no prompt. P4's failed task showed people didn't come across it naturally.

After

Immersive mode became a progressive step, offered with a gentle prompt once a reader has generated at least one visual.

2

Visual intensity controls

Before

A three-state toggle (Off / Low / Medium) felt too binary. Participants wanted to say how much visual support they needed.

After

A continuous slider with labelled stops (Off → Subtle → Moderate → Rich), plus a preview thumbnail.
